The first marks

Before words,
there were images.

Deep inside caves, early humans traced hands, hunted animals, and left signs whose meanings are still mysterious. Art began as ritual, memory, map, and magic—all at once.

Animals, handprints, earth pigments

𓁶

Gods, rulers, order

Art builds an
eternal world.

From Egypt to Greece and Rome, art gave visible form to power and belief. Ideal bodies, monumental architecture, and strict visual systems promised order against the chaos of time.

Look for
Monuments, ideal forms, mythology
Touchstones
Nefertiti · Parthenon · Pantheon

03
c. 400–1400
✦

Light made sacred

Heaven enters
the picture.

Medieval art turned away from earthly realism toward spiritual meaning. Gold grounds, illuminated pages, and soaring cathedrals made the invisible feel present.

Look for
Gold leaf, icons, stained glass
Touchstones
Book of Kells · Chartres Cathedral
The world opens ↓

A new humanism

The world gains
dimension.

Artists studied anatomy, nature, antiquity, and light. Linear perspective opened a window into believable space, while the individual—artist and subject alike—stepped into focus.

Look for
Perspective, anatomy, balance
Artists
Leonardo · Michelangelo · Raphael

05
c. 1600–1750
B

Drama in motion

Turn up
the emotion.

Baroque art stages the instant everything changes. Bodies twist, fabric surges, and a single shaft of light cuts through darkness. The viewer is no longer outside the scene—they are in it.

Look for
Movement, contrast, theatricality
Artists
Caravaggio · Bernini · Artemisia
Reason returns ↓
06
c. 1750–1850

RATIO

Virtue & reason

Order becomes
revolutionary.

Inspired by archaeology and Enlightenment ideals, artists revived the clean lines and moral gravity of antiquity. Art became a stage for civic duty, sacrifice, and the new republic.

Look for
Clarity, restraint, classical dress
Artists
Jacques-Louis David · Angelica Kauffman
Feeling rebels ↓
07
c. 1780–1850

The sublime unleashed

Reason meets
the wild.

Romantic artists chose awe over order and feeling over rules. Storms, revolutions, ruins, and untamed landscapes reveal nature—and human emotion—as beautiful, terrifying forces.

Look for
Nature, intensity, the sublime
Artists
Turner · Goya · Caspar David Friedrich
Eyes turn outward ↓
08
c. 1840–1880

HERE
& NOW

Life without a filter

The everyday
takes center stage.

No gods, no heroes—just laborers, city streets, and ordinary life. Realists insisted that the present was worthy of serious art and used observation as a form of social truth.

Look for
Work, daily life, social critique
Artists
Courbet · Millet · Rosa Bonheur
Light begins to move ↓
09
c. 1860–1886

Painting the instant

Catch the light
before it changes.

Portable paint tubes and modern city life took artists outdoors. Broken brushstrokes and pure color captured fleeting weather, leisure, and the sensation of seeing in real time.

Look for
Visible brushwork, light, modern leisure
Artists
Monet · Morisot · Degas
Color breaks free ↓
10
c. 1886–1905
✦

Beyond appearances

Color finds
an inner life.

There was no single next step. Cézanne built nature from shapes, Van Gogh charged it with emotion, Seurat systematized color, and Gauguin sought symbolic worlds.

Look for
Structure, symbolism, expressive color
Artists
Van Gogh · Cézanne · Seurat
The rules dissolve ↓

c. 1905–1970

FORM
FREED

Make it new

Art reinvents
its own language.

Photography, war, machines, and radical politics fractured old certainties. From Cubism to abstraction, Dada to Pop, artists questioned not just how to make art—but what art could be.

Movements
Fauvism · Cubism · Surrealism · Pop
Artists
Matisse · Picasso · Kahlo · Warhol

the medium.

Installation, performance, video, code, social practice, painting—the contemporary field holds them all. Artists work across cultures and disciplines to question identity, power, technology, and our shared future.
