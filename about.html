About the project

A guided argument, not a neutral timeline.

Art Compass is an independent educational introduction to art history. It connects visual form to history, belief, culture, economics, politics, technology, literature, architecture, and design.

Who this is for

Art Compass is written primarily for curious adult learners and beginning college-level survey students. It assumes no prior art-history vocabulary, but it asks readers to compare evidence, distinguish interpretation from fact, and continue into museum and scholarly sources. It is a starting point—not a replacement for a course, collection visit, or peer-reviewed research.

Image policy: Works-and-makers sections use locally stored public-domain or Creative Commons reproductions where reuse was verified. Every reproduction displays its license, image credit, and provenance link. Clearly labeled AI-generated studies remain where reusable imagery was not confirmed.

Editorial approach

Selection

The twelve chapters form a deliberately concise teaching sequence. Featured works and texts were selected because they make a period’s central visual or intellectual shift legible to a general reader.

Interpretation

Period boundaries are approximate. Categories such as “Medieval,” “Renaissance,” and “Modernism” are historical frameworks developed in particular scholarly traditions, not natural divisions shared by every culture.

Cause and consequence

Each chapter distinguishes conditions that shaped art from changes art helped produce. These relationships are interpretive and should not be read as single-cause explanations.

Writing and review

The material is written for general education. It has not yet received formal peer review by a credentialed art historian; readers should consult the cited museum, archive, and scholarly sources for research use.

Artwork and generated imagery

Local reproductions are included only when an image record identifies them as public domain or Creative Commons. A work’s age alone is not treated as permission to reuse a modern photograph of it.

Generated images demonstrate visual principles while avoiding unlicensed reuse of protected or uncertain works. They may simplify or combine historical characteristics and must be read with their labels. Chapter, architecture, and design environments remain editorial interpretations rather than documentary evidence.

Geographic method

Maps use present-day Natural Earth country boundaries only as orientation. Darker highlights mark representative centers associated with the chapter; lighter highlights indicate wider exchange, adaptation, or influence. They do not reproduce historical borders, claim uniform participation across a modern country, or imply that unhighlighted societies lacked significant art.

Known limitations

  • The twelve-part sequence remains weighted toward European art-history periodization.
  • Large, internally diverse regions are necessarily compressed.
  • Digital reproductions cannot reproduce the material presence of original objects.
  • Maps show broad orientation rather than precise historical networks.
  • Dates, attributions, and interpretations may change with scholarship.
